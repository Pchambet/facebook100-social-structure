Network science report · Facebook100 (September 2005)

What organises friendship on a campus?

100 complete campus friendship graphs, 1,208,316 accounts and 46,984,537 friendships: which attributes friends share, how predictable new ties are, and whether the network's communities follow dorms or class years.

1. Who befriends whom

Newman's assortativity coefficient r measures how much more often friends share an attribute than random mixing would predict (0 = no preference). Missing values are excluded, as in Traud et al. (2012).

Class year dominates on 91 of 100 campuses (median r = 0.44), followed by status and dorm (median 0.22). Major (0.05), gender (0.06) and high school (0.03) barely structure the network; degree assortativity is weak (0.07).

Erratum

The first version of this project read local_info with six columns instead of the documented seven (it skipped "second major / minor") and counted missing values as a category. What it called "year" was the dorm, and what it called "dorm" was the minor. The table recomputes both versions in the same run.

Consequence: the original conclusion "dorms barely matter" was an artefact. Dorms do matter (mean r 0.227, not 0.019); class year matters 1.9 times more.

2. Size, density and clustering

Campuses range from 769 to 41,554 accounts, but the mean number of friends only ranges 39-116 (median 78). Density therefore falls with size (correlation of logs -0.97), while transitivity stays around 0.16, about 19 times the density, the signature of a clustered, non-random network.

3. Predicting hidden friendships

10% of edges are hidden at random (5 splits on each of the 10 smallest campuses). The common benchmark (AUC against random non-edges) is compared with the realistic task: rank every unlinked pair, as a "people you may know" feature would.

The four common-neighbour scores reach AUC 0.94 or more on the easy benchmark; on the full ranking the best (Resource allocation) gets 22.9% of its top-k pairs right, about 57 times the 0.40% base rate. The original claim of 96% precision@100 came from ranking hidden edges against a sample of ten random non-edges per hidden edge, not against every candidate.

The graph alone recovers class year far above the baseline and dorms substantially. Major stays hard (26%) and gender gains only 7 points, consistent with their weak assortativity.

5. Do communities follow dorms or years?

Louvain communities on the 50 campuses with at most 10,000 accounts, compared with each attribute by the adjusted Rand index (0 = chance).

Communities follow class year on 46 of 50 campuses. The exceptions: Caltech36 (dorm), Rice31 (dorm), Smith60 (dorm), UCSC68 (dorm). Caltech is the case highlighted by Traud et al.: its communities match the residential houses (ARI 0.69) and not the class year (ARI 0.01).

Method notes and limitations

  • Data: Facebook100, a single snapshot from September 2005, intra-school links only. Attributes are self-reported and partly missing; missing values are excluded per attribute.
  • Assortativity is descriptive: it does not separate homophily (choosing similar friends) from shared context (living in the same dorm, taking the same classes).
  • Link prediction hides edges uniformly at random, which is easier than predicting future ties. Full-ranking metrics are computed on the 10 smallest campuses only (dense n x n matrices).
  • Louvain is run once per campus (seed 0) and only on campuses with at most 10,000 nodes.
